# Objections on early sales calls

Load this while prepping, or when the founder pastes something a prospect just said. Each objection below has the usual readings, the one question that tells them apart, how an honest answer is built, what not to say, and when to stop selling.

## How to answer any objection

1. **Say it back in their words.** "So the worry is you'd be paying for two tools that overlap." It shows you heard it and stops you answering the wrong thing.
2. **Ask one question before answering.** Most objections have two or three possible meanings. Don't guess which one you're facing.
3. **Answer with their words or real evidence.** The best material is what they told you 15 minutes earlier. Next best is something you can show on screen right now. A claim you can't back up is worse than no answer.
4. **Check.** "Does that cover it?" If not, there's a second objection behind the first. Ask about it.

"I don't know, I'll find out and tell you by Thursday" is allowed and often builds more trust than a smooth answer. Then send it by Thursday.

Never, in any answer: invent a customer or a result, quote a competitor's price or feature you haven't checked today, promise a roadmap date your cofounder hasn't agreed to, or claim a certification you don't hold.

## "We already use X"

**Usual readings:**
- X covers the job well enough and switching isn't worth it.
- X could do it in theory, but nobody has set it up, so the job still lives in a spreadsheet.
- They're worried about paying twice, or about adding one more login for the team.

**Question that tells them apart:** "When you did <the job> last month, where did it actually happen? In X, or somewhere else?" Ask them to show you, if the call allows.

**Honest answer:** if the job happens in X and works, say so and stop selling ("Then you probably don't need us, and I'd rather know now"). If it happens outside X, the gap is in their own words: "You said the status still lives in a sheet the partner can't see. That's the part we do." Say clearly whether you replace X or sit beside it.

**Don't say:** anything about what X can't do unless you've checked it in X's current docs or seen it on their screen. "I haven't looked at how X handles that recently" is honest. Never run X down; they chose it.

**Walk away when:** they run the job in X, it works, and nobody is unhappy about it.

## "It's too expensive"

**Usual readings:**
- Compared to something: another tool, a part-timer, doing nothing.
- They don't yet believe it solves the problem, so any price feels high.
- The price is fine but the buyer isn't on the call, or the budget sits with someone else.
- A real cash limit.

**Question that tells them apart:** "Too expensive compared to what?" Then let them answer.

**Honest answer:** go back to the cost they named in discovery. "You said chasing status took most of a day at month-end. That's the comparison I'd make." Only use their number. If they never named a cost, discovery isn't done; ask now. If it's a real cash limit, offer a smaller start (one team, one use, a shorter paid pilot), not a lower price for the same thing.

**Don't say:** a made-up ROI figure, or a discount offered on the spot. A quick discount teaches them the first price wasn't real.

**Walk away when:** the problem costs them less than your price, by their own account.

## "Not now" / "Maybe next quarter"

**Usual readings:**
- Something real is in the way: a busy season, a hire, a migration.
- It isn't painful enough to move this quarter.
- A polite no.

**Question that tells them apart:** "What happens between now and then that makes it a better time?" A real reason has a date. A polite no doesn't.

**Honest answer:** for a real reason, agree a dated check-in and put it in the calendar now, plus one small step before then if it costs them nothing (send last month's tracker, a 15-minute look with the ops lead). For no reason, ask directly: "Is it timing, or is it not a big enough problem right now? Either is fine to say."

**Don't say:** "Prices go up next month" unless it's true and was already decided.

**Walk away when:** there's no reason and no date. Mark it Stalled, not a deal.

## "I need to ask my partner" (or boss, or the team)

**Usual readings:**
- They really can't decide alone.
- They're keen but unsure how to sell it internally.
- A soft way out.

**Question that tells them apart:** "What do you think they'll ask?" and "If it were your call alone, what would you do?"

**Honest answer:** offer to be in the room. "Could we do 20 minutes with them this week? I'll keep it to the part you said matters most." If they'd rather ask alone, give them a three-line summary they can forward, in their words (template in `call-kit.md`), and agree when you'll hear back.

**Don't say:** anything that goes around them to the partner without asking. They are your way in.

**Walk away when:** they won't name a date to talk to the partner and won't bring you in. Mark it Stalled.

## "What if you go away?" (you're small, you're new)

**Usual readings:**
- Fear of losing their data or their setup.
- Fear of putting work into a tool that vanishes.
- Doubt that you'll still answer emails in six months.

**Question that tells them apart:** "What would you lose if we disappeared next year?"

**Honest answer:** be plain about the facts, and only the facts: how long you've been at it, who's on the team, and how much runway you have if the founder is comfortable sharing it. Then make it safe: they can export everything at any time, in a format they already use, and you'll say so in writing. Month-to-month terms if that helps. Being small also means they talk to the founder, not a queue; offer your direct line.

**Don't say:** anything about funding, investors or customers you don't have. Don't promise "we'll be around forever".

**Walk away when:** they need a vendor with years of history and a big support team. That's a real fit gap; say so kindly.

## "Send me something"

**Usual readings:**
- Genuine: they want to show someone or read it later.
- A polite way to end the call.

**Question that tells them apart:** "Happy to. Who's it for, and what would they need to see in it?"

**Honest answer:** if there's a real reader, send a short note within the day (under 120 words, their pain in their words, the next step) and book the follow-up before hanging up: "I'll send it this afternoon. Can we take 15 minutes on Friday to go over what they thought?" If there's no reader and no date, it's a polite no. Ask: "Is it worth us talking again, or is this not the right time?"

**Don't say:** "I'll send our deck." A generic deck is the opposite of what they just told you.

**Walk away when:** they want the document but won't agree to any follow-up. Send it once, then mark it Stalled.

## When it isn't really an objection

- **A feature request.** "Does it do Y?" Answer yes or no, then ask what they'd use it for. Write it down. Two or more prospects asking for the same thing is input for `feature-spec`; one is a note.
- **A question you can't answer.** Security, data location, integrations: "I don't know off-hand. I'll check and send it by <day>." Then do.
- **A real fit gap.** Say it out loud. Qualifying out cleanly keeps the door open for later and keeps your pipeline honest.
