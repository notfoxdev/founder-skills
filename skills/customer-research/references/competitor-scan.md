# Competitor and alternatives scan reference

The goal is not a list of logos. It is an answer to: what does the target customer do today, why does it fall short for them, and what would it take for them to switch.

## Four groups

| Group | Definition | Example (hypothetical: scheduling for small clinics) |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Same job, same approach, same segment | Clinic scheduling software |
| Indirect | Same job, different approach or broader tool | General calendar apps, practice-management suites with a scheduling module |
| DIY / status quo | Manual or improvised | Paper book, shared spreadsheet, a receptionist on the phone |
| Do nothing | Live with the problem | Accept no-shows as a cost of business |

Rule: include at least one row for DIY / status quo and one for do nothing. If interviews show most customers use the status quo, say so first; it changes the positioning job from "better than X" to "worth changing for".

## Verification protocol

When search or fetch tools are available:
1. For each alternative, fetch the pricing page, homepage, and one recent changelog or release notes page if present.
2. Record for each fact: the claim, the URL, and the access date.
3. Prices: copy the plan names and listed prices as shown, including currency and billing period. If pricing says "contact sales", record that; do not estimate.
4. Positioning: copy their headline or tagline as their own words, in quotation marks, with the URL. Keep quotes short.
5. Funding, headcount, customer counts: only from a source you fetched. Otherwise `[UNVERIFIED]`.
6. If two sources disagree, record both and flag it.

When tools are not available: state that at the top of the output, fill only what the founder supplied, and mark everything else `[UNVERIFIED — check before use]`. Provide the founder with the exact pages to check.

## Where to find weakness evidence

| Source | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Review sites (G2, Capterra, Trustpilot, app store reviews) | 1–3 star reviews from the target segment; recurring complaints; "switched from" mentions |
| Community threads (Reddit, niche forums, Slack groups) | "Alternatives to X" threads; complaints about price, complexity, support |
| Competitor support docs and status pages | Gaps admitted in docs, known limitations, frequent incidents |
| Job postings | What they're investing in next |
| Archived versions of their site | Positioning changes over time (signals what didn't work) |
| The founder's own interviews | Why customers left or never adopted it; strongest source for this segment |

Rules:
- A weakness needs at least one cited source. Two independent sources make it a pattern.
- Note the reviewer's segment when visible. A weakness for enterprise buyers may be irrelevant to a 5-person team.
- Summarise complaints in your own words; quote at most a short phrase with its link.

## Switching cost

Score Low / Medium / High and name the dominant factor:
- **Data:** history, records, or content that must be migrated.
- **Workflow:** other tools and processes built around it.
- **Contract:** annual terms, cancellation fees, procurement cycles.
- **Learning:** time to retrain the team.
- **Habit and social:** "everyone here knows it", a champion who chose it.

High switching cost from an incumbent means the new product needs a sharp, specific advantage or a segment the incumbent serves badly. Against the status quo, switching cost is often the effort of starting anything at all; a concierge or done-for-you start lowers it.

## Common mistakes

- Listing only funded startups and missing the spreadsheet most customers actually use.
- Treating feature checklists as positioning; customers buy outcomes.
- Concluding "no competitors" (it almost always means the problem isn't painful or the search was too narrow).
- Copying a competitor's claims as facts about their quality.
