# Copy frameworks

Read this when setting the message hierarchy, choosing a headline angle, or sizing sections. Frameworks are scaffolding. The test is always whether a specific reader would act.

## Awareness levels

From Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966). How much the reader already knows decides where the page starts.

| Level | Reader knows | Page opens with | Section order bias |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unaware | Nothing; does not see a problem | A situation or cost they recognise | `problem` early, story-led |
| Problem-aware | The pain, not that solutions exist | The pain, named precisely | `problem` -> `how-it-works` -> `proof` |
| Solution-aware | Solutions exist, not yours | Your mechanism or difference vs the alternative | `how-it-works` -> `proof` -> `features` |
| Product-aware | Your product, not convinced | Proof, specifics, risk reversal | `proof` -> `pricing` -> `faq` |
| Most-aware | Ready, needs a reason now | The offer and CTA | `hero` -> `pricing` -> `final-cta` |

Most early-stage pages serve problem-aware or solution-aware readers. Cold traffic from ads and communities skews less aware than traffic from a founder's own posts.

## Forces of progress

From the jobs-to-be-done "switch" interviews (Bob Moesta, Chris Spiek). A buyer switches when push + pull outweigh anxiety + habit.

| Force | Question | Where it lands on the page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Push | What is going wrong today? | `problem` |
| Pull | What would better look like? | `hero` subhead, `how-it-works` |
| Anxiety | What could go wrong if they switch? | `faq`, under-CTA line, `pricing` |
| Habit | Why will they keep doing what they do? | `problem` (name the workaround), `how-it-works` (show switching is small) |

Most weak pages over-invest in pull (features) and ignore anxiety and habit.

## Section patterns and word budgets

Budgets are ceilings for a typical early-stage page, not targets.

| Section | Pattern | Budget |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `hero` headline | One claim the reader can picture | 4–10 words |
| `hero` subhead | Who it is for + how it works or what changes | 15–30 words |
| `hero` CTA | Verb + object + (optional) what happens next | 2–5 words |
| Under-CTA line | Removes one anxiety ("No card required", "Takes 3 minutes") only if true | 3–8 words |
| `proof` | Logos, one quote, or one sourced number; or an honest stage statement | 0–40 words |
| `problem` | Scene from their day, the workaround, the cost | 40–90 words |
| `how-it-works` | 3–4 steps, each a verb-led title + one sentence | 15–25 words per step |
| `features` | Capability -> outcome, 3–6 items | 15–35 words each |
| `pricing` | Tier name, price, who it is for, 3–6 inclusions, CTA | 30–60 words per tier |
| `faq` | 5–8 objections, direct answer first | 20–60 words per answer |
| `final-cta` | Restate the shift + CTA + reason to act now | 15–35 words |

Reading level: aim for grade 6–8 (Hemingway or Flesch-Kincaid). Technical audiences can take jargon they use themselves; they still prefer short sentences.

## Headline angles

Write each option from a different angle. Templates show structure only; fill with the brief's specifics.

| Angle | Structure | Works when | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Outcome | [Concrete result] [for whom / without what] | The result is specific and believable | Vague outcomes ("grow faster") |
| Problem | [The pain, in their words] | Readers are problem-aware and the pain is sharp | Reads negative; needs a strong subhead |
| Contrast with the alternative | [Job] without [the current workaround] | The alternative is well known and hated (spreadsheets, agencies, manual review) | Sounds like a negation tell if phrased "not X but Y" |
| Audience call-out | [Job] for [narrow audience] | The audience is narrow and proud of it | Excludes adjacent buyers (often fine early) |
| Mechanism | [How it works, made concrete] | Solution-aware, technical, or sceptical buyers | Too clever; reader cannot picture it |
| Time or effort | [Job] in [real time] | You have measured the time | Invented numbers |

Good subheads do the work the headline cannot: name the reader, name the mechanism, or give the proof.

## Problem section

Structure that works for most B2B and many B2C pages:

1. **Scene.** A specific moment in their week ("It is Thursday, payroll closes Friday, and two timesheets are still missing").
2. **Workaround.** What they do now and why it half-works.
3. **Cost.** What the workaround costs in time, money, risk, or reputation. Only real or founder-confirmed figures.

Avoid generic agitation ("Frustrated? Overwhelmed?"). Name the thing.

## How it works

- 3 steps is the default; 4 if setup is a real step the reader worries about.
- Each step: a verb-led title and one sentence with a concrete detail.
- Step 1 should feel small (connect, upload, invite). That answers habit and anxiety.
- If the product is pre-launch, describe what it will do and say so ("In the beta, you will...").

## Features vs benefits

Write capability -> consequence -> evidence:

- Capability: what it does ("Reads supplier invoices from your inbox").
- Consequence: what changes for the reader ("Nobody retypes line items").
- Evidence: a number, a screenshot reference, or nothing if you have none.

Cut any feature the reader would not use to decide.

## Proof hierarchy

Strongest to weakest. Use the strongest you actually have.

1. Named customer result with permission (person, company, metric, timeframe).
2. Recognisable customer logos with permission.
3. Named quote about a specific outcome.
4. Usage numbers with source and date.
5. Design partners or pilot count, stated honestly ("Built with 5 dental practices in Leeds").
6. Founder credibility relevant to the problem ("Built by two former hospital schedulers").
7. A transparent stage statement ("Private beta. We read every reply.").

Never: invented quotes, "trusted by thousands" without a number and source, review stars without a source.

## Objections and FAQ

Source objections from, in order: sales and discovery calls, support questions, competitor reviews, the alternatives in `company.md`, the founder's guesses (marked `[ASSUMPTION]`).

Common categories to check:

- Will it work for my setup? (integrations, size, industry)
- How long until it is useful? (setup time, learning curve)
- What does it cost, really? (seats, overages, contracts)
- What if it goes wrong? (data safety, support, cancellation, export)
- Why trust a new company? (who is behind it, what happens if you shut down)
- Why not just keep doing what I do?

Answer first, then explain. "Yes. You can export everything as CSV at any time." not "Great question! We believe in data portability..."

## CTAs

- Name the action and the object: "Start a free trial", "Book a 20-minute demo", "Get early access".
- Match commitment to awareness: low-awareness pages ask for less (email, template, demo video) than product-aware pages (trial, purchase).
- One primary CTA repeated in `hero` and `final-cta` with the same wording. A secondary CTA (watch demo, see pricing) as a text link.
- Avoid: "Submit", "Learn more", "Get started" with nothing after it, "Sign up now!".

## Final CTA

Restate the shift in one line, then the CTA, then a reason to act now that is true: a beta closing, a price for early customers, a cost of waiting the reader recognises. If there is no true urgency, do not invent one; repeat the lowest-friction next step instead.

## Meta title and description

- Title 50–60 characters: `<Primary phrase or outcome> | <Brand>`.
- Description 140–160 characters: outcome + audience + action. Write for the person scanning search results, not for keyword stuffing.
- Count characters and report the counts.

## Voice-of-customer mining

When the founder has raw material (call transcripts, reviews, tickets):

1. Pull exact phrases about the problem, the workaround, and the moment they started looking.
2. Group into push, pull, anxiety, habit.
3. Prefer repeated phrases. One customer's vivid phrase is a quote; five customers' shared phrase is a headline candidate.
4. Keep the source next to each phrase so it can be cited or verified.
