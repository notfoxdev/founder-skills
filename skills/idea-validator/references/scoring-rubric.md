# Scoring rubric

Read before scoring in Mode A. Two rules govern every score:

1. **Evidence caps.** The type of evidence sets the maximum score. Argument, enthusiasm, or market size cannot exceed the cap.
2. **Count people, not mentions.** One interviewee saying something five times is one data point. Conversations must be with the target segment and about past behaviour to count.

## Evidence caps

| Evidence type | Max score | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| None, or founder belief | 1 | "Obviously people need this" |
| Founder's own experience | 2 | One data point, heavily biased |
| Desk research (reports, forums, search trends, competitor existence) | 3 for H1, H5, H6; 2 for H2–H4 | Shows the topic exists, not that your segment has the pain |
| Fewer than 5 past-behaviour interviews | 3 | Directional only |
| 5+ past-behaviour interviews with consistent pattern | 4 | The working ceiling without commitments |
| Commitments (money, LOI, data handed over, intro to buyer, repeat usage) | 5 | Behaviour that costs the customer something |

A "consistent pattern" means at least 3 people, and at least 40% of those interviewed in the segment, independently describe the same thing from a specific past event.

## Anchors per hypothesis

### H1 Customer
- 1: Described as a demographic or "everyone who...".
- 2: A role or segment, but the founder cannot name people in it.
- 3: Segment defined by role + context + trigger; founder can name 10+ reachable people.
- 4: Interviews show this segment has the pain more sharply than adjacent ones.
- 5: People in this segment have committed (paid, signed up for a pilot with real data, introduced the buyer).

### H2 Problem
- 1: Problem stated in the founder's words only.
- 2: Founder has experienced it; or forums discuss it.
- 3: 1–4 interviewees described it from a specific recent event.
- 4: Consistent pattern across 5+ interviews, described before the founder raised it or with detail the founder did not supply.
- 5: People act to get it solved: chase the founder, offer to pay, hand over data.

### H3 Frequency and intensity
Acute pain is any one of: occurs weekly or more; or costs a measurable amount of money or hours each time; or has visible consequences (lost revenue, compliance exposure, a person blamed).
- 1: Unknown.
- 2: Occasional, or annoying but tolerated.
- 3: Some interviewees meet the acute-pain definition.
- 4: The pattern meets the acute-pain definition and people have a workaround they spend time or money on.
- 5: People are paying for a workaround now and are dissatisfied with it, with specifics.

### H4 Willingness to pay
- 1: No pricing discussion; or "it should be free".
- 2: Hypothetical pricing only ("I'd pay $20").
- 3: Existing spend on the problem identified (tools, contractors, staff time) and the payer's budget located.
- 4: Several prospects discussed a specific price without objection and named the approval process.
- 5: Money or a signed LOI with a price.

### H5 Channel
- 1: "Social media", "ads", "it'll go viral".
- 2: Named channel, untested.
- 3: Founder reached 10+ target customers through the channel for interviews.
- 4: Channel produced conversations or signups at a rate the founder has measured.
- 5: Channel produced paying customers at a cost the founder has measured and can afford.

### H6 Why now
- 1: No answer.
- 2: General trend ("AI is big").
- 3: A specific, dated change (regulation, API availability, cost drop, platform shift) with a public source the founder should verify.
- 4: Interviewees cite that change as the reason their situation is different now.
- 5: Customers are actively searching for a solution because of that change.

### H7 Why you
- 1: No relevant access, insight, or skill, and low interest.
- 2: Interest and general skills.
- 3: Domain experience or a network that reaches the segment.
- 4: Unusual insight or access competitors lack (proprietary data, distribution, years in the role).
- 5: Demonstrated: the founder has already reached and sold to this segment.

## Problem profile (Mode A, step 2)

Rate the problem, never the product. Evidence caps still apply: desk research alone can make "popular" or "growing" at most "partly"; the founder's own experience alone makes any rating "partly" at best.

| Question | "Yes" means | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| Popular | Many people or companies in the segment have it; you can name 10+ and estimate the total from a source the founder can check | H1 |
| Growing | The number who have it is rising (new regulation, more firms, a shift in behaviour) with a dated source | H6 |
| Urgent | It has to be dealt with now: a deadline, a customer waiting, money leaving | H3 |
| Expensive | Each occurrence costs measurable money or hours, or people already pay to work around it | H3, H4 |
| Mandatory | Something outside their choice requires it: law, a client contract, the business can't run without it | H3 |
| Frequent | It happens weekly or monthly, not yearly | H3 |

Reading it: urgent plus expensive or mandatory is a painkiller. All three "no" is a nice-to-have, and H3 caps at 2. Popular and growing without urgency is the "wide, not deep" pattern. Don't add up the yeses; one strong "mandatory" can carry an idea that's only moderately frequent.

## Red flags (Mode A, step 4)

| Flag | Test | Clears when |
|---|---|---|
| Solution in search of a problem | Ask: "Who was the last person to hit this, and what happened?" If the honest answer starts with the technology ("AI could...") or a product ("an app that..."), it's present | 3+ people in the segment describe the problem from a specific past event without the founder prompting the solution |
| Tarpit | List the companies that tried this and why they stalled. Consumer coordination ideas ("find things to do with friends") are the classic case: easy to build, hard to get people to change behaviour. Present if several attempts died for the same structural reason and the founder can't say what's different now | The founder names the attempts, the shared reason they died, and a dated change or insight that removes it; or a search finds no meaningful failed attempts |
| Made-up idea | Does the founder want it? Does anyone they know want it badly enough to try a rough version this month? Present if the answer is "people would probably like it" | One named person outside the founder's circle asks for it or commits time |
| Wide, not deep | Count people who need it now versus people mildly interested. Present when the only signals are aggregate (waitlist, likes, survey "yes" rates) | 5+ people who need it now, ideally in one tight group, where you can serve them by hand |

Questions worth asking in any pressure test, mapped to hypotheses: founder–market fit (H7); how acute is it (H3); who else does this (red team; having competitors is usually fine); do you or people you know want it (made-up check); what changed recently to make it possible (H6); is there a proxy company with a similar model that shows the model can work (desk evidence for H4 or H5, cap 3); would you stay in this problem space for years (H7 KILL clause); and is it easy to change if the first version is wrong (lowers the cost of a KEEP TESTING).

Ideas that are noticed rather than brainstormed (the founder hit the problem while doing the job) usually clear the first and third flags. A problem that's tedious to solve ("schlep") is not a red flag; it often keeps competitors away.

## Reading the total

Do not sum scores into a single number for the verdict; the verdict rules in SKILL.md use individual hypotheses. A sum hides a fatal 1 behind several 5s. You may report the profile ("strong problem, no channel") as a summary.

## Worked example (hypothetical, for calibration only)

Founder: "Invoice-chasing tool for freelance designers." Evidence: 6 interviews, no commitments.
- H2: 4 of 6 described chasing a late invoice in the last month, unprompted, with amounts. Score 4 (pattern, interviews).
- H3: 3 of 6 spend 2+ hours a month on it; 1 pays a virtual assistant. Score 4.
- H4: Nobody discussed price; one said "I'd pay for that". Score 2 (hypothetical).
- H5: All 6 came from one Slack community. Score 3.
- Verdict path: not PROVISIONAL (6 conversations). Not KILL (pain is present). H2 and H3 are 4 and H4 is 2, so the PIVOT rule appears to match. But price was never asked in any interview, so the 2 reflects missing evidence, not negative evidence. Verdict: KEEP TESTING, with a pricing conversation in the next 5 interviews as the first test, and an explicit note that the PIVOT rule was considered and not applied.

Rule from the example: a score of 2 caused by a question never asked is missing evidence. A score of 2 after the question was asked and answered poorly is negative evidence. Only negative evidence triggers PIVOT or KILL.
