# Customer conversations that produce evidence

Source of the method: Rob Fitzpatrick, "The Mom Test: How to talk to customers and learn if your business is a good idea when everyone is lying to you" (2013). This file summarises the approach in original wording for use by agents. Founders should read the book; nothing here reproduces its text.

The core premise: people are polite. If you describe your idea, most will say something encouraging, and that encouragement predicts nothing. Useful conversations are about the customer's life and recent behaviour, so that politeness has nothing to latch onto.

## Rules

1. **Talk about their world, not your idea.** Until the last few minutes, the idea should not come up. Ask about the problem area, their workflow, and what they did recently.
2. **Ask about specific past events, not opinions or the future.** "Tell me about the last time" beats "Would you ever". Past behaviour is the only thing that has already cost them something.
3. **Listen more than you talk.** In a 30-minute call the founder should speak less than a third of the time. Silence after an answer often brings out the useful detail.
4. **Deflect compliments.** When someone says "that sounds great", steer back: "Thanks. How are you handling it today?"
5. **Anchor generics.** "I usually..." or "I always..." gets "When did that last happen? Walk me through it."
6. **Dig under feature requests.** "It should have Slack integration" gets "What would that let you do? How do you handle that now?" The request is a clue to a problem, not a spec.
7. **Dig under emotion.** "It drives me crazy" gets "What happened last time? What did it cost you?"
8. **Ask what they've already tried.** If they have never searched for a solution, spent money, or built a workaround, the problem may not be painful enough.
9. **Find the money.** Who owns the budget, what do they pay for today in this area, how do purchases get approved.
10. **Ask for a commitment at the end.** A conversation without a clear next step is usually a polite dead end.
11. **Keep it casual.** A coffee-style conversation gets franker answers than a formal "user interview".
12. **Write it down the same day,** using the shorthand below. Unrecorded conversations do not count as evidence.

## Bad question to good question

| Avoid | Ask instead | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Would you use a tool that does X? | How do you handle X today? | Hypothetical vs. actual behaviour |
| Do you think this is a good idea? | What's the hardest part of X for you? | Opinion vs. their priorities |
| How much would you pay for this? | What do you currently spend on this, in money or hours? | Guess vs. existing spend |
| Would you buy this if it existed? | What have you tried so far to fix it? | Promise vs. evidence of effort |
| Do you ever have trouble with X? | Tell me about the last time X happened. | Yes/no leading vs. a story |
| What features would you want? | What would let you stop doing the thing you do now? | Wish list vs. outcome |
| Is X a problem for you? | What are the top three things that slow you down in Y? | Leads them vs. lets them rank |
| How often does this happen? | When did it last happen? And the time before? | Estimate vs. dated events |
| Would your team adopt this? | How did your team pick the tool you use now? | Speculation vs. buying process |
| Don't you hate when X? | How do you feel about how X works now? | Puts words in their mouth |
| Can I show you a demo? (in minute 2) | (Hold the demo until the last 5 minutes, if at all) | Pitching ends learning |
| Would you recommend it to others? | Who else do you know who deals with this? | Hypothetical vs. a real referral |

## Statement types

| Type | Markers | Evidence value | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fact about past behaviour | dates, amounts, names of tools, "last week I..." | High | Record verbatim |
| Pain signal | emotion or cost tied to a specific event | High when paired with a fact | Ask what it cost |
| Generic | "usually", "always", "never", "typically" | Low | Anchor to the last instance |
| Hypothetical | "would", "will", "might", "I'd pay" | None | Convert into a commitment ask |
| Compliment | "love it", "great idea", "I'd totally use that" | None | Deflect and return to their world |
| Feature request / opinion | "it should...", "you need..." | Low | Ask what it would let them do |

## Commitment ladder

Commitments cost the customer something. They are the strongest evidence a conversation can produce. Three currencies: time, reputation, money.

| Level | Example | Currency |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | "Keep me posted" / "Sounds cool" | none (polite exit) |
| 1 | Agrees to a specific follow-up meeting with a date | time |
| 2 | Shares real data, files, or access to their workflow; joins a trial using real work | time |
| 3 | Introduces a peer, their boss, or the budget holder | reputation |
| 4 | Agrees to be a named pilot customer or public reference | reputation |
| 5 | Signs an LOI with a price, pays a deposit, or preorders | money |

Decision rules:
- At idea stage, ask for level 1–3. At validation and MVP stage, ask for level 3–5.
- Record both the ask and the response. "Asked for intro to their manager, declined" is useful negative evidence.
- If you asked for nothing, record "no ask". That is an interviewer error, not a customer signal.
- A conversation that ends with "keep me posted" is level 0, however warm it felt.

## Signs a conversation went badly

- You got compliments and no facts.
- You described the idea before minute 20.
- You can't name one thing they did in the last month related to the problem.
- You learned nothing that surprised you.
- There is no next step on either side.

## Note-taking shorthand

Prefix each line in notes so analysis is fast:
- `F:` fact about past behaviour
- `P:` pain (with cost if known)
- `W:` workaround currently used
- `$:` money spent or budget mentioned
- `H:` hypothetical or future claim
- `C:` compliment
- `R:` feature request
- `K:` commitment asked / given
- `?:` follow-up to ask next time

Quotes go in quotation marks, word for word. Paraphrase goes without them. Never upgrade a paraphrase into a quote later.
