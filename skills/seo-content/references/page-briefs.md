# Page briefs

The brief template, what each early page type needs, how to get the founder's experience onto the page, and the checks before anything is published. Read this in steps 4–7.

A brief is short on purpose. If it takes more than half an hour to fill, the page isn't clear yet.

## The full brief

```markdown
## Brief — <working title>
**Reader:** <role, firm size, the moment they're in when they search>
**Phrase in their words:** "<what they'd type>" (evidence: <n of N interviews / call / thread link>)
**What they want when they search it:** <learn how / get a template / compare options / define a term>
**Volume:** <n/month, tool, date> or [NEED: ...]
**What the top results already say:** <2–3 lines, checked on <date>>
**What ours adds:** <one sentence; if blank, drop the page>
**Answer in two sentences:** <the thing a reader should walk away with even if they stop here>
**First-hand material:**
- Story: <a real situation the founder lived, with what happened>
- Numbers: <ones the founder actually has, with source> or [NEED: ...]
- Artefact: <template, checklist, screenshot of a sheet with client names removed>
**Outline:** H1 <...> | H2 <...> | H2 <...> | H2 <...>
**Links in (from):** <existing pages that should link here>
**Links out (to):** <our pages, outside sources cited>
**CTA:** <one action that fits the reader's stage>
**Title (<n> chars):** <...>
**Meta description (<n> chars):** <...>
**URL:** /<short-words-from-the-phrase>
**Must not claim:** <features not shipped, competitor facts not checked, numbers not measured>
**Byline:** <founder name, one line on why they know this>
```

## What each page type needs

| Type | Must contain | Usual structure | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem page | The situation in the buyer's words; what causes it; what it costs; what people try first | Name it → why it happens → what it costs → what works → where we fit (one short section) | Turning into a sales page by paragraph two |
| How-to for the job | Real steps someone can follow today, with or without our product | Answer first → steps → the mistakes the founder saw → a worked case → optional "doing this in <product>" | Steps so generic any tool's blog could have written them |
| Template or checklist | A usable download or copyable list, plus how to use it and where it stops working | What it's for → the template → how to fill it → when you'll outgrow it | Gating it behind a form before the reader has seen it |
| Comparison or alternatives | Who should pick the competitor, where we fit, dated side-by-side facts | Built from the `competitor-teardown` page outline | Claims about the competitor with no date or source |
| Glossary | A plain definition in the first two sentences, then why it matters to this buyer | Definition → example from the job → related terms | Writing for students when the buyer already knows the term |
| Founder experience | What the founder did, for how long, what they learned, what they got wrong | Story → three or four lessons with specifics → what we're building and why | Reading like a bio instead of something useful |

Rules for every type:
- The answer sits in the first paragraph. A partner reading between calls should get it in ten seconds.
- One page, one phrase and intent. If two pages chase the same phrase, merge them.
- Our product gets one short section at most, near the end, and only describes what exists.
- Dates on anything that changes: tool steps, prices, competitor facts. "Last checked <date>" is fine.

## Getting the founder's experience onto the page

This is the part nobody else can write. Spend 20 minutes asking the founder, and take notes in their words. Ask about one real time, not general opinions.

1. "Tell me about the worst time this went wrong. What happened, step by step?"
2. "What did you try first? What did you end up doing?"
3. "What did you know after a year that you didn't know in month one?"
4. "What do people outside the job get wrong about it?"
5. "Is there a number you remember? Hours, clients, days late. How do you know it?"
6. "Did you build a sheet, a checklist or a rule of thumb? Can we show it with names removed?"

Use what they say, close to how they said it. A remembered number is "about", with the founder as the source. Don't round it up or make it precise.

## Titles and meta descriptions

- Title: the phrase near the front, what the reader gets, about 50–60 characters so it isn't cut off. Brand at the end only if it fits.
- Meta description: about 140–160 characters; what the page answers and for whom. It isn't a ranking factor, it's the line that earns the click. Search engines sometimes rewrite it.
- H1: one per page, close to the title, written for the reader.
- Report the character count of each draft.
- No title written to bait a click the page doesn't pay off.

## Internal links for the first 5–8 pages

- Every page links to the founder experience page (byline) and to one or two related pages, with link text that says where it goes ("our month-end close checklist", not "click here").
- The template page and the main how-to link to each other.
- The homepage or waitlist page links to the two strongest pages.
- No page is left with zero links pointing at it.

## Before it goes live

Read the draft three ways:
1. **As the sharpest customer.** A partner who has done this job for ten years. Does any line state the obvious, oversell, or sound like a brochure? Rewrite or cut it.
2. **As the founder talking.** Read it out loud. If a sentence wouldn't come out of their mouth across a table, rewrite it in their words.
3. **As a fact-checker.** Every number has a source. Every outside fact has a link. Nothing describes a feature that isn't shipped. Nothing about a competitor is undated.

Then strip the AI tells listed in CONVENTIONS (and `human-voice` if installed): "it's not X, it's Y" contrasts, forced threes, staged openers, tidy closing lines, stacked em dashes, "delve", "robust", "in today's fast-paced world".
