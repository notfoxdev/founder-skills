# Market category strategies

Read this when choosing the market category (frame of reference) in step 5. The category tells buyers who you compete with, what features to expect, and roughly what you cost. Choosing it is a strategic decision, not a naming exercise.

## What a category does

When a buyer hears a category label, they instantly assume:
- **Competitors:** who else they should look at.
- **Features:** what any product in this category must have.
- **Price range:** what this kind of thing usually costs.
- **Buyer:** whose job it is to buy it.

Good category choice makes your unique value look like an obvious strength. Bad choice makes it invisible or makes you look like you are missing table-stakes features.

## Strategy 1: Head-to-head

Compete directly in an existing, understood category and aim to win it.

Use when:
- The category is well understood and buyers already search and budget for it.
- You can win on the criteria buyers already use to choose, or
- The category is young and no leader has been established yet.

Avoid when:
- An entrenched leader defines the buying criteria and you are weaker on them.
- Your best value sits outside the category's usual criteria.

What it requires:
- Parity on table-stakes features buyers expect.
- A clear, provable edge on at least one top buying criterion.
- Willingness to be compared on feature grids.

Messaging shape: "The <category> that <wins on criterion>." Proof must be comparative and specific.

## Strategy 2: Big fish, small pond

Take an existing category and narrow it to a sub-segment whose needs the leader underserves. Win that sub-segment decisively, then expand.

Use when:
- An existing category roughly fits, but a specific segment has distinct needs (industry, size, geography, workflow, compliance).
- You have attributes that matter a lot to that segment and little to the general market.
- The segment is reachable and large enough to build a first business.

Avoid when:
- The segment's needs are not actually different (you are just narrowing the audience, not the value).
- The segment is too small to support the business and there is no plausible adjacent segment.

What it requires:
- A segment-qualified category label: "<category> for <segment>" or "<segment>-specific <category>".
- Proof from that segment.
- A view of the next adjacent segment.

Messaging shape: "<Category> built for <segment>." Buyers in the segment self-select; buyers outside it are fine to lose.

This is the default for most early-stage startups because it reduces head-on competition, sharpens messaging, and makes channel targeting easier.

## Strategy 3: New category

Define and lead a category that does not yet exist.

Use when all are true:
- Every existing category label misleads buyers about what you do or invites the wrong comparison.
- Buyers recognise the problem but have no budget line or name for the solution.
- You can fund a long education effort (content, events, analyst relations, evangelism) over years.
- You have early evidence that buyers adopt the new framing.

Avoid when:
- An existing category, perhaps narrowed, works well enough.
- The motivation is to sound bigger or avoid competitors.
- Runway is short.

What it requires:
- A clear, plain category name buyers can repeat.
- A point of view on why the old way fails now (often tied to a trend).
- Patience: buyers do not search for or budget for categories that do not exist.

Most category-creation attempts fail or are absorbed into an existing category. Treat it as the exception.

## Decision guide

Answer in order. Stop at the first "yes".

1. Is there an existing category where your value is obvious and buyers already shop? → **Head-to-head**, if you can win on its criteria.
2. Is there an existing category that fits, but a reachable segment underserved by it values your attributes much more? → **Big fish, small pond**.
3. Does every existing category mislead buyers, and can you fund education? → **New category**.
4. None? → Revisit best-fit customers and value themes; the components are not settled yet.

## Testing a category choice

- **Expectation check:** list the competitors, features, and price a buyer will assume for the label. If any of these hurt you, adjust.
- **Comprehension check:** show the one-liner to 5 people in the target segment. Ask what the product is, who it is for, and what it would replace. Count correct answers.
- **Search check:** do best-fit customers use this label when they look for solutions? Founder can check search tools and community language; do not guess volumes.
- **Sales check:** use the category in the opening line of 10 sales or discovery conversations. Note whether buyers lean in or ask "so it's like <wrong thing>?"

## Trends

A trend helps when it explains why the problem is newly urgent or why now is the time to switch. Connect it in one sentence: "<trend> means <best-fit customer> now faces <problem>, which <product> solves by <attribute>."

Reject trends that:
- are popular but unrelated to the value themes,
- the target buyer does not experience directly,
- make the company look like it is chasing hype.
