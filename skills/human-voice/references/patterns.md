# Pattern catalogue

The canonical list of tells for the whole pack. Other skills point here instead of keeping their own lists. Run it over the full text, not sentence by sentence: several of these only show up at paragraph scale.

How to read each entry:
- **Strength.** Strong: one sighting justifies an edit. Weak: a careful human does this on purpose often enough that you act only when a second tell sits in the same paragraph.
- **Fix.** Go back to the fact underneath and write that. Swapping a tell for a synonym makes a new tell.
- Every before and after below is invented to show the shape of a fix. None of it is real company data.

## 1. Content tells

What the text claims, or pretends to claim.

**1.1 Ordinary news dressed up as history** (strong). Ordinary news dressed as a turning point: "marks a pivotal moment", "a major milestone", "sets the stage for".
- Before: "Signing our first design partner marks a pivotal milestone in our journey to transform how clinics schedule staff."
- After: "Our first design partner, a four-clinic physio group, starts using the rota tool on Monday."

**1.2 Unnamed experts and outlet lists** (strong). Unnamed experts, "studies show", "industry reports", or a list of outlets standing in for evidence.
- Before: "Experts agree that manual invoicing is costing small agencies thousands every year."
- After: "Three of the agency owners I interviewed said they chase at least one late invoice a week." (If no source exists: keep the claim and add `[NEED: source]`.)

**1.3 Claims ahead of the evidence** (strong). "Customers love it", "teams are seeing huge results", "trusted by" when the company has a waitlist or free pilots. Flag; never soften into "may help".
- Before: "Early users are already saving hours every week."
- After: "Early users are already saving hours every week [NEED: who, how many, measured how]." Suggest the honest version beside it: "Two beta users have run their weekly report in it so far."

**1.4 Vague quantities** (strong). "Countless", "thousands of founders", "a growing number of teams", "many".
- Before: "Countless agencies struggle with scope creep."
- After: "Scope creep came up in 7 of my 10 calls with agency owners." Or cut it.

**1.5 Feelings with no event** (weak). "Humbled", "thrilled", "excited to share", "beyond grateful" with nothing concrete attached.
- Before: "I'm incredibly humbled and excited to share some amazing news with you all."
- After: "We have our first two pilot customers." (The news is the excitement.)

**1.6 The send-off** (strong). A final paragraph about the future, the journey, or "exciting times ahead". End on the last concrete thing: the next step, the date, the ask.
- Before: "This is just the beginning, and we can't wait to see what the future holds."
- After: "We start on 3 March. I'll post what we learn in April."

**1.7 Benefit filler** (strong). "So you can focus on what matters", "giving you peace of mind", "so you can do more of what you love".
- Before: "Automate your payroll so you can focus on what really matters."
- After: "Payroll runs on the 25th without you opening a spreadsheet."

## 2. Language tells

Words and phrases models reach for far more than people do.

**2.1 Stock AI vocabulary** (strong in groups, weak alone). delve, robust (outside engineering), seamless, leverage, elevate, unlock, unleash, harness, empower, streamline, supercharge, landscape, realm, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, game-changer, cutting-edge, next-generation, journey, embark, navigate (as a metaphor), foster, showcase, underscore, meticulous, vibrant, holistic, synergy.
- Before: "We leverage cutting-edge AI to streamline your workflow and unlock seamless collaboration."
- After: "It reads your support inbox and tags each ticket by product area before your team logs in."

**2.2 Fancy verbs standing in for "is" and "has"** (weak). "Serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features", "represents".
- Before: "The dashboard serves as a central hub and boasts over 40 integrations."
- After: "The dashboard has 40 integrations." (Then check that 40 is true.)

**2.3 Tacked-on -ing clauses** (strong). A clause bolted on after a comma to make a fact sound deeper: "..., ensuring nothing slips", "..., empowering teams to...".
- Before: "Reminders go out automatically, ensuring every client stays informed and engaged."
- After: "Clients get a reminder two days before each session."

**2.4 Stacked hedges** (weak). "Could potentially help", "may possibly", "in some cases might". Keep one hedge if the doubt is real.
- Before: "This could potentially help reduce churn in some cases."
- After: "We think this reduces churn. We'll know after the March cohort."

**2.5 Corporate verbs and nominalizations** (weak). "Utilize", "facilitate", "make a decision", "provide assistance", "drive alignment".
- Before: "We facilitate the onboarding process for new hires."
- After: "We set up a new hire's laptop, accounts and first-week calendar."

**2.6 Fuzzy relationships** (weak). "In connection with", "associated with", "tied to" when the text could say how.
- Before: "Our founder is associated with several leading fintech companies."
- After: "Our founder was an engineer at two payments startups." (Only if the founder said so.)

**2.7 Intensifier padding** (weak). "Truly", "incredibly", "deeply", "extremely", "super".
- Before: "We're truly, deeply committed to our users."
- After: Cut it. Show the commitment with a fact elsewhere or not at all.

## 3. Structure tells

The shape of sentences and paragraphs. These outlast word swaps, so check them twice.

**3.1 "Not X, but Y"** (strong). In all its forms: "It's not just X, it's Y", "X isn't about A, it's about B", the split version ("This isn't a tool. It's a teammate."), and the clipped tail ("..., no guesswork"). Keep a contrast only when the reader really believes X.
- Before: "Ledgers isn't just accounting software. It's a growth partner for your business."
- After: "It shows which invoices will be late before they are." (Needs a confirmed feature.)
- Before (clipped tail): "Setup takes five minutes, no engineers required."
- After: "Setup takes five minutes. You paste one line into your site's header."

**3.2 Forced lists of three** (strong when repeated, weak once). Three adjectives, three benefits, three examples, whether or not there are three things.
- Before: "Built for speed, simplicity, and scale."
- After: "Loads a 10,000-row export in under a second." (Pick the one that is true and specific.)

**3.3 Tidy closers and fragment rows** (strong). A short paragraph that repeats the point ("That's the power of focus."), a row of fragments ("No setup. No contracts. No stress."), or "Read that again."
- Before: "We cut onboarding from two weeks to two days. That's the difference."
- After: "We cut onboarding from two weeks to two days." (Stop there.)

**3.4 Staged openers and reveals** (strong). "Here's the thing:", "Let's dive in", "Let's be honest", "The result?", "The best part:", "Spoiler:", "Picture this:".
- Before: "The result? Our pilot clinic filled every Saturday shift."
- After: "Our pilot clinic filled every Saturday shift in February."

**3.5 Answering an objection nobody raised** (strong). "To be clear", "This isn't to say", "Don't get me wrong", "You might think..., but" when nobody said it.
- Before: "Don't get me wrong, spreadsheets are great. But they weren't built for this."
- After: "The three agencies I interviewed each kept project scope in a different doc, and none of them knew which one was current."

**3.6 Mirrored pairs** (strong). "Less admin. More selling." "Fewer meetings, better decisions."
- Before: "Less chasing. More closing."
- After: "Reps see which deals have had no reply in 7 days."

**3.7 Same-shape sentences** (weak). Every sentence the same length, or several in a row starting with "We". People vary; let a short line follow a long one.
- Before: "We started in March. We talked to 30 owners. We built a prototype. We launched in June."
- After: "We started in March and spent six weeks talking to 30 owners before building anything. The prototype went out in June."

**3.8 Heading restated as first line** (weak). "## Pricing" followed by "Pricing is important." Delete the echo.

## 4. Formatting tells

**4.1 Em dash everywhere** (weak alone, strong at two or more per paragraph). Use a full stop, comma, colon or parentheses. If the founder's own samples use dashes, match their rate.
- Before: "Our first hire — a designer — starts Monday — and we're hiring two more."
- After: "Our first hire, a designer, starts Monday. We're hiring two more."

**4.2 Bold labels on every line** (strong). "**Speed:** ... **Security:** ... **Support:** ..." Turn into sentences unless it is a reference table.

**4.3 Broetry** (strong on LinkedIn). One sentence per line, every line its own paragraph, for the whole post. Group into short paragraphs of 1–3 sentences.

**4.4 Emoji bullets and decoration** (strong). Rockets, check marks and arrows as bullets or before headings. Remove them; the pack bans emoji.

**4.5 Hashtag piles** (strong). Eight hashtags, or hashtags mid-sentence. Keep 0–3 at the end, only ones the founder's audience actually follows.

**4.6 Title Case Headings And Subject Lines** (weak). Use sentence case unless the brand uses title case.

**4.7 Exclamation marks** (strong in founder writing). Confidence comes from the fact. One in a personal thank-you is a human choice; three in a post is a tell.

**4.8 Curly quotes in plain-text channels** (weak). Fine in documents, odd in a terminal or a commit message.

## 5. Tone and register tells

**5.1 Assistant leftovers** (strong). "I hope this helps", "Great question", "Certainly", "Feel free to reach out", "Let me know if you have any questions" pasted into the final text.
- Before: "Great question. Our pricing starts at $49 per month. Let me know if you'd like more details."
- After: "It's $49 a month per location. Want me to send the annual option too?"

**5.2 Over-formal for the channel** (strong). "I am writing to inform you", "Please be advised", "We would like to take this opportunity" in an email to a user.
- Before: "We would like to take this opportunity to inform you that your trial will expire shortly."
- After: "Your trial ends on Friday."

**5.3 Fake intimacy** (strong). "Hey friend", "Real talk", "Can I be vulnerable for a sec?" from someone the reader does not know.
- Before: "Real talk: building a startup is hard."
- After: Cut it, or say the specific hard thing: "We lost our biggest pilot in week three."

**5.4 The corporate "we"** (weak). "Our team is committed to excellence" from a two-person company. Say who did what.
- Before: "Our team has worked tirelessly to deliver this release."
- After: "Sam rebuilt the importer over two weekends."

**5.5 Sycophantic replies** (strong). "Absolutely, that's a great point" before disagreeing, or praise before every answer.
- Before: "Absolutely, great point. You're so right that onboarding matters. That said, ..."
- After: "Agreed on onboarding. I'd still ship the import first, because ..."

**5.6 Hype register** (strong). "Revolutionary", "game-changing", "the future of", "redefining".
- Before: "We're redefining the future of field service."
- After: "Technicians get the job sheet on their phone before they reach the van."

**5.7 Manufactured urgency** (strong). "Don't miss out", "spots are filling fast", "only a few left" unless true and countable.
- Before: "Spots are filling fast, so grab yours today."
- After: "We have room for 5 more firms in the November cohort. 2 are taken." (Only if true.)

**5.8 Email autopilot** (strong). "I hope this email finds you well", "Just circling back", "Quick question", a fake "Re:" on a first message, a sign-off that offers help in general instead of asking for one thing.
- Before: "Hi there, I hope this finds you well. Just circling back on my last note. Feel free to reach out with any questions."
- After: "Hi Dana, did the import finish on your side? If it stalled, reply with the file name and I'll look tonight."

## 6. Writing for the wrong reader

**6.1 Recapping what the reader already knows** (strong in replies). A reply that restates the question, the history and the reasoning before the answer.
- Before: "Thanks for raising the CSV import issue. As you know, many users upload large files, and our importer has some limits, so after looking into it we've decided to raise the limit."
- After: "We've raised the import limit to 50,000 rows. It's live now."

**6.2 The buried ask** (strong). The one thing the reader should do sits in the last paragraph.
- Before: Four paragraphs of update, then "Also, could you intro me to your head of ops?"
- After: Open with the ask, then the two lines of context that make it reasonable.

**6.3 Writing to "the user"** (strong). "Users can leverage the dashboard" in text sent to users. Write "you".

**6.4 Wrong level of jargon** (weak). Explaining "MRR" to investors, or saying "reconciliation variance workflow" to a first-time visitor. Match what this reader already knows.

**6.5 Writing about the text** (strong). "In this post, I'll share three lessons", "Below is a summary of". Start with the first lesson.
- Before: "In this post I want to share a few thoughts on what we learned from our first 10 sales calls."
- After: "Eight of our first 10 sales calls ended on the same question: does it work with Xero?"

**6.6 Answering a question nobody asked** (weak). A paragraph defending the price, the stack, or the team when the reader asked about the date.

## When to leave it alone

- Quotes, titles, product names, and passages that discuss a phrase rather than use it.
- A habit the founder's own samples show (dashes, a triad, a favourite word). The sample wins.
- Three items when there really are three things.
- Greetings and sign-offs in letters and emails. "Thanks, Maya" is not a tell.
- Odd specific details, asides, self-corrections and mixed feelings. Those are the voice.
- Legal or compliance wording.
- A short line that carries a new fact. Short is fine; empty is the problem.

## Final pass

1. Diff against the fact ledger: nothing added, nothing dropped without a note.
2. Search for "not ", "just", "isn't", "—", "!", "Here's", "journey", and the 2.1 list.
3. Count lists of three and one-line paragraphs. More than one of each per short piece: rewrite one.
4. Swap test: put a competitor's name in. Lines that still work need a specific or get cut.
5. Read it as the sharpest person in the audience would, then out loud once.
